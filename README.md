# Thank you, Harvey Richer

I first met Harvey Richer in January 2012. He was my professor for a course that's no longer taught at UBC. From what I recall, it was offered every two years and whoever taught it could teach about anything they chose. Naturally, we learned all about globular clusters that year.

It was a wonderful course. We had projects going through classic Harlow Shapley papers on globular clusters. It was a pretty small class, so we all had an opportunity to prepare a 1.5hr lecture. I was fascinated by some of the dynamics that Harvey had taught us and I wanted to dive into some more details for my lecture. He let me borrow his copy of Binney & Tremaine over reading week. I brought it with me on a trip to visit two friends in Toronto and Halifax. Perfect light reading for a plane. That was my first introduction to more complex astronomical dynamics and I loved it. As an aside, I'm pretty sure I talked about dynamical friction during my general astrophysics qualifying exam (or at least, I attempted to; but I did pass unconditionally).

I had tried to land a summer job doing astronomy research, but unfortunately I didn't get anything. Quite late in the term, I went to Harvey's office one day (his bright, warm, and welcoming office) and asked him if he had any summer research work for a student. Alas, he didn't have any funding. I was quite desperate and offered to work for free. He declined (politely). Students need to be paid for their work. Still, he called and emailed some people he knew to find someone who could take me on. It was too close to the start of summer, but I was so grateful that he tried.

That September, I started a co-op job at TRIUMF doing physics research. I spent the year there working on two different projects. I learned that while I love learning physics, I didn't love that kind of physics research. I started the final year of undergrad thinking that I wasn't going to go to grad school and that I just didn't like doing research.

I was in an honours programme and had to do a thesis, however. I needed to find a supervisor. Because of my previous interactions with Harvey, he was one of my top choices. He had a project for a student to try to detect a white dwarf pileup in globular clusters and compare luminosity functions with cooling models to detect white dwarf core crystallisation. He and Jeremy Heyl supervised me.

Harvey had beautiful Hubble data of 47 Tuc and I used a collaborator's proper motion data to obtain a phenomenal white dwarf luminosity function. I had a clear signal of a change in the rate of cooling. Jeremy helped me learn MESA. The luminosity function seemed to match my core crystallisation models. I spent the summer continuing to work with Jeremy on modelling white dwarfs in MESA to check for other possible causes. Unfortunately, I couldn't figure out how to get some things to work (and spent some time investigating axions as an alternative to banging my head against the wall). Later, Ilaria Caiazzo completed that modelling. For our white dwarfs in 47 Tuc, core crystallisation occurs at approximately the same time as convective coupling kicks in and is the dominant effect for the observed change in the luminosity function. I'm quite proud of that work! Here's the paper if anyone would like to see it: https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..677O/abstract

He was an incredibly kind and supportive mentor. At graduation, he and Jeremy were on stage waiting to congratulate me as I walked across. Harvey gave me a big hug. My parents were so proud of me. My supervisors showing up for me let them see that I wasn't just another student in the sea of convocation. They were proud of me, too. (PS: if you have any general thoughts about professors hugging students, I probably agree; but I'm still grieving a dear mentor of mine and this is a fond memory I will always cherish)

Harvey had such a great sense of humour. Once I asked him if a photo in his office was of him and his grandson. Straight-faced, he said that was him and his son. Professors only have time to start a family very late in life. He couldn't keep it up for too long and started laughing. He was kind and funny and made me feel like I was a colleague of his, not just a student.

But Harvey also had high expectations of me. He knew I was capable of doing great research. I remember once I showed up to our weekly meeting having not made any progress (I'd probably been quite busy working on assignments and studying for midterms). He was still kind, but he let me know that he was disappointed. This was time we'd set aside to discuss my project. It was important to come prepared with something new or a problem I was stuck on. I needed to do the work.

I had so much fun working with Harvey (and Jeremy!) during my final year. I started in September thinking that research wasn't for me and that I wouldn't go to grad school. I graduated in May knowing that I was going to do a PhD. I was torn between doing more research on white dwarfs or switching to planetary science. I ended up going to UofT for a PhD, doing a mini project supervised by Christa van Laerhoven, and falling in love with planetary dynamics. I still kept in touch with Harvey and I visited him at UBC a few times when I was back in Vancouver. I attended A Richer Universe, a celebration of his 75th birthday in 2019. It was lovely to hear from and share stories with so many other people who worked with and learned from Harvey.

Gwendolyn Eadie joined UofT in 2019. While I can't speak for her, I could tell that Harvey's mentorship influenced her teaching and mentorship philosophy. Because I knew it influenced mine. I think Harvey left a mark on his students. He made me also want to be the kind of person who can guide students and junior colleagues to their fullest potential through kindness and encouragement. By motivating them to do their best work and helping them get there. He showed me that my work as a student was meaningful and important and that it had value. Students aren't a source of free or cheap labour. He was training astronomers and he took that part of the job seriously.

I defended my PhD in August 2023. I travelled home to Vancouver only a couple of days later.  I emailed several mentors of mine to tell them that I was now Dr. Obertas (and also to thank them), including Harvey. I offered to meet up with him while I was there. Sadly, a family member had unexpectedly passed away only the week before my defence. It was a bit of a chaotic time and I didn't get around to responding to his email. I went back to Toronto and finished up my revisions. I was so burnt out (amongst other things, I wrote ~60 pages of my thesis in about 2.5 months) that I just needed to check out for a while. I figured I'd have time to reply. That I'd be able to see him again. I thought we'd have a chance to celebrate.

He passed away in November 2023 and I think I will always regret not seeing him one last time. I don't think he'd want me to carry that, but I know I will. I wouldn't be the person and the astronomer I am today without him. Thank you for everything, Harvey.
